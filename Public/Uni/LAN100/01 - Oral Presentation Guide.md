# Oral Presentation: My Future Goals

A step-by-step guide to the LAN 100 oral presentation, built from the professor's slide deck ([[1. LAN_100_Future_Goals_Oral_Presentation_Slide_Deck.pptx|Future Goals Oral Presentation deck]]). It covers what to present, how to research and cite it, how to build the slides, how to deliver the talk, and how it is graded.

> [!important] At a glance
> - **Weight:** 10% of the grade · **Individual**
> - **Length:** <span class="hl-yellow">4–5 minutes</span>
> - **PowerPoint required**, with a final **References** slide (APA 7)
> - **Content:** 4 future goals (Academic, Career, Hobby/Interest, Free Choice), each with **one researched fact from a documented source**
> - **Language:** English only, at **B2** level
> - **Key principle:** *"The PowerPoint supports the speaker; it does not replace the speaker."*

---

## 1. The Task

> [!note] The task in one sentence
> Present **four future goals** and explain each one clearly **in your own words**. Each goal must include **one relevant, researched piece of information from a documented source**.

The professor sums it up as:

**Presentation = personal ambition + useful information + clear spoken explanation**

The research part keeps it from being just a general personal talk. Every goal needs a small, documented fact that you found and can explain.

## 2. The Four Required Goals

Goals should be **specific, meaningful and future-focused**. They can be realistic or imaginative, short-term or long-term.

| Goal | What it covers | Example ideas | Possible researched fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Academic** | study, degree, qualification, learning aim | finish your BSc with a good GPA, master's abroad, PhD, a certification (e.g. AWS, Cisco), learning a new language to C1 | programme length, entry requirements, a specific course or research area, a language exam's levels |
| **Career** | future profession, workplace, internship | software engineer, data scientist, work at a specific company, a summer internship, starting your own business | what the role involves, required skills, a company's internship programme, job growth figures from a government statistics site |
| **Hobby / Interest** | skill, activity, passion, project | run a half-marathon, learn guitar, build a game, get a diving licence, photography | training plan length from a recognised organisation, licence levels, official event information |
| **Free Choice** | travel, service, personal growth, community | travel to Japan, volunteer, learn to cook, get fitter, help your community | visa/entry info from an official site, a volunteering organisation's programme, health guidelines (e.g. WHO activity recommendations) |

> [!tip] Choose goals you can actually talk about
> You will speak for about a minute on each goal without reading. Pick goals you **genuinely care about** and can explain with real reasons. A real goal is easier to explain than an impressive one you don't believe in.

> [!warning] Edge cases with goals
> - **Overlapping goals:** "Master's in AI" (Academic) and "Become an AI engineer" (Career) are related but still **two different goals**. That's fine, as long as each has its own explanation and its **own** researched fact. Don't reuse the same fact or source for both.
> - **Free Choice must not repeat another category.** "Get a better job" as Free Choice is just a second Career goal. Use it for something different (travel, health, service, personal growth).
> - **"I don't know what I want to do yet."** That's allowed. Choose a goal you're *considering* and say so: *"One option I'm seriously considering is…"*. It's still a goal, as long as you explain it and research it.
> - **Too vague:** "I want to be successful" or "I want to travel" is not specific. Make it concrete: *which* degree, *which* job, *which* country, *by when*.
> - **Too private:** you don't have to share anything you're uncomfortable with. Pick goals you're happy to talk about in front of the class.

## 3. Each Goal Has Three Parts

| # | Part | What to do | Example (Academic goal) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ambition** | State the goal clearly, in one sentence. | *"I would like to complete a master's degree in environmental engineering."* |
| 2 | **Explanation** | Explain **why** it matters to you and **how** you might achieve it. | *"I'm interested in climate solutions, and I want to work on practical water or energy projects. My plan is to…"* |
| 3 | **Research** | Add **one useful fact** from a **reliable documented source**, paraphrased and cited. | *"According to the university's website, the programme includes courses on sustainable infrastructure and water management (University name, 2026)."* |

> [!example]- Professor's example: "I want to study for a PhD"
> Turn the ambition into research questions:
> - **Which university?**
> - **What does the programme focus on?**
> - **What requirement or research area is relevant?**
>
> Answer **one** of these with a documented source. That answer is your researched fact.

> [!tip] A good "fact" is specific and checkable
> - ✅ *"The programme lasts 18 months and requires a bachelor's degree in engineering."*
> - ✅ *"The WHO recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week for adults."*
> - ❌ *"Engineering is a good career."* This is an opinion, not a fact.
> - ❌ *"Many people like Japan."* Too general, and no source would say it in a useful way.

## 4. Timing and Slide Plan

You have **4–5 minutes**. At a normal speaking pace (about 120–140 words per minute), that's roughly **550–650 spoken words** in total.

![[Oral Presentation - Timing Plan.png|800]]

| Slide | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title:** "My Future Goals", your name, LAN 100 | — |
| 1–2 | **Introduction:** greet, introduce yourself and preview the 4 goals | ~30 s |
| 3 | **Academic goal:** ambition + why/how + fact (citation) | ~55 s |
| 4 | **Career goal** | ~55 s |
| 5 | **Hobby / Interest goal** | ~55 s |
| 6 | **Free Choice goal** | ~55 s |
| 7 | **Conclusion:** short summary + how the goals connect + thank the audience | ~20 s |
| 8 | **References** (APA 7), shown, not read aloud | — |

That gives about **8 slides** and roughly **4:30**, leaving ~30 s of buffer for nerves, pauses or a slower pace.

> [!warning] Timing traps
> - **Under 4 minutes** usually means the explanations are too thin. Add a *how* (steps, timeline) or a personal example to each goal.
> - **Over 5 minutes** usually means you're reading too much detail. Cut the explanation, not the researched fact.
> - **Nerves make you faster.** If you finish at exactly 4:00 at home, you'll probably finish at 3:30 in class. Aim for **4:30** when rehearsing.
> - Time yourself **out loud, standing up**. Reading in your head is much faster than speaking.

## 5. Research: Finding a Good Source

The professor asks for **reliable sources**:

| ✅ Use | ⚠️ Use with care | ❌ Don't use as the source |
|---|---|---|
| **University** websites (programme pages, admissions) | **Wikipedia**: good for finding the real source in its references; cite *that* instead | **ChatGPT / any AI tool** (explicitly forbidden as a factual source) |
| **Professional** bodies (e.g. engineering associations, IEEE, ACM) | **News articles** from reputable outlets: fine for recent facts, but check the date | Personal blogs, forums (Reddit, Quora) |
| **Government** websites (statistics offices, official travel/visa info) | **Company websites**: fine for facts *about that company* (e.g. its internship programme), not for general claims | Social media posts, TikTok, Instagram |
| **Recognised organisations** (WHO, UNESCO, the official body of a sport) | | Anonymous pages with no author or organisation |

> [!tip] Quick reliability check
> Ask: **Who wrote it?** (a named organisation or expert) · **When?** (is there a date, and is it recent?) · **Why?** (to inform, or to sell you something?). This connects to *Author's Purpose* from the midterm material ([[LAN100_Midterm_Revision.pdf|Midterm revision notes]]): a source written **to inform** is better than one written **to persuade** or sell.

> [!warning] Research edge cases
> - **The source is in Greek.** You can use it, but you must **present the fact in English** (paraphrased). In the APA reference, keep the original title and add an English translation in square brackets: *Τίτλος σελίδας [Page title in English]*.
> - **AI found the source for you.** That's acceptable *preparation* (brainstorming), but you must **open the real page yourself**, check that it says what you claim, and cite **that page**. AI tools often invent sources and facts.
> - **The fact is a number** (salary, statistic). Say where it applies and when: *"In Cyprus, in 2024…"*. Don't present a US salary as if it applies everywhere.
> - **One source for all four goals.** Avoid it. Each goal should have its **own** relevant source.

## 6. APA 7 Referencing

The professor expects **a consistent attempt at APA 7**, appropriate for B2 level. Perfection isn't required, but consistency is. You need two things:

1. A **short in-text citation** on the slide where the fact appears.
2. A **References slide** at the end, with a full entry for each source.

> [!note] In-text citation (on the goal slide)
> **(Author, Year)**. The author is often an **organisation** for websites.
> - (University of Cyprus, 2025)
> - (World Health Organization, 2024)
> - **No date on the page →** (Organisation, n.d.). "n.d." means *no date*.

> [!note] Reference entry for a webpage (on the References slide)
> **Author. (Year, Month Day). *Title of page*. Site Name. URL**
>
> - **When the author and the site name are the same** (very common, e.g. a university writing on its own site), **leave out the site name**:
>   Organisation Name. (Year). *Title of page*. URL
> - **No date →** use (n.d.)
> - The **page title is in italics**. The **site name is not**.

> [!example]- Worked examples (illustrative: replace with your real pages)
> **Organisation is the author and the site (site name omitted):**
> University Name. (n.d.). *MSc in Environmental Engineering*. https://www.university.example/msc-environmental-engineering
>
> **Named person on an organisation's site:**
> Surname, A. B. (2025, March 3). *Title of the article*. Site Name. https://www.site.example/article
>
> **Non-English page:**
> Organisation Name. (2024). *Τίτλος σελίδας [Page title in English]*. https://www.site.example/page
>
> In-text versions: (University Name, n.d.) · (Surname, 2025) · (Organisation Name, 2024)

> [!important] References slide rules
> - Title the slide **References** (not "Bibliography" or "Sources").
> - Put the entries in **alphabetical order** by the first word (author surname or organisation name).
> - Use a **hanging indent** (second and later lines indented). In PowerPoint: Paragraph → Indentation → Special: *Hanging*.
> - **Every in-text citation must have a reference entry, and every entry must be cited** on a slide. Check this both ways.
> - Official help: [APA reference examples](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/references/examples)

> [!warning] Common APA mistakes
> - Citing **"ChatGPT"** or **"Google"** as the source. Google is a search engine, not a source; cite the page you found.
> - Putting only a **bare URL** on the References slide with no author, date or title.
> - **Different formats** for different entries. Consistency is exactly what's being graded.
> - A full reference squeezed onto the goal slide instead of a short (Author, Year).
> - Forgetting **(n.d.)** and inventing a year, or using the year you visited the page.

## 7. Speaking in Your Own Words: Paraphrasing

The research must be **paraphrased**, not read out word for word from the source. This is the *Paraphrasing* skill from the midterm material.

> [!note] Paraphrasing
> Expressing a source's information **in your own words** while **keeping the original meaning**, and still acknowledging the source.

**Six-step process** (from the midterm revision notes):
1. Read until you understand it.
2. Identify the essential meaning and key terms.
3. **Look away** from the original.
4. Rewrite the idea in a new sentence (here: *say* it).
5. Compare the two versions.
6. Check that the meaning is accurate and the wording is different enough.

> [!example]- Paraphrase example
> **Source:** *"The programme equips students with advanced knowledge in the design and management of sustainable water systems."*
>
> - ❌ **Weak:** "The programme gives students advanced knowledge in the design and management of sustainable water systems." *(only one word changed, same structure)*
> - ✅ **Good:** "According to the university, students on this programme learn how to design and run water systems that are sustainable." *(new structure, same meaning, source named)*

**Phrases for introducing a source out loud:**
- *"According to [the university's website / the WHO]…"*
- *"The source explains that…"*
- *"I found on [organisation]'s website that…"*
- *"Research from [organisation] shows that…"*
- *"As [organisation] points out,…"*

> [!tip] Say the source, show the citation
> Name the source **out loud** in a natural way (*"According to the university's website…"*) **and** show the (Author, Year) citation on the slide. You don't need to read "open bracket, University of Cyprus, 2025" aloud.

## 8. Building the Slides

> [!important] The rule
> Make the slides **attractive, clear and simple**. The slides show the key information; **you** explain the story behind them.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Short headings, **keywords**, brief bullet points | Put the **full speech** on the slides |
| **Relevant visuals:** photos, maps, logos, diagrams | Use random decorative images or clip-art |
| **Check spelling** carefully (a spelling mistake on a big screen is very visible) | Use tiny fonts or low-contrast colours |
| One goal per slide, same layout for all 4 goals | Change the design on every slide |
| Short citation on each goal slide + final **References** slide | Forget the References slide |

![[Oral Presentation - Slide vs Speech.png|800]]

> [!example]- Professor's model goal slide
> **Academic Goal**
> I would like to complete a master's degree in environmental engineering.
>
> **Why this goal matters to me**
> - I am interested in climate solutions.
> - I want to work on practical water or energy projects.
>
> **Researched information**
> A university programme I found includes courses connected to sustainable infrastructure and water management. (University name, 2026)
>
> *Slide = keywords only. The spoken explanation gives the detail.*

> [!tip] Practical slide tips
> - **Font size:** at least 24 pt for bullets and 36 pt+ for titles, so it's readable from the back of the room.
> - **Max ~6 lines** per slide. If you need more, it's probably your script, not your slide.
> - **Images:** use logos/photos of the actual university, company, place or activity. A photo of the university's campus is better than a generic "graduation hat".
> - **Image credit:** if you use someone else's photo, a small credit line (e.g. *Image: University Name*) is good academic practice, even though the task doesn't require it.
> - Use **speaker notes** in PowerPoint for your own keywords, but don't read them like a script.

## 9. Explain, Don't Read

The assessment is about **spoken communication, not slide-reading**. You should:
- speak **in your own words**
- **paraphrase** researched information
- refer to sources **naturally**
- use **prompt cards only if needed**
- show you **understand every slide**

> [!warning] What counts as reading
> - Turning your back to the audience to read the screen.
> - Reading a memorised script word for word in a flat voice. It sounds read even if you aren't looking at anything.
> - Reading full sentences from your phone or cards.
>
> **Prompt cards are allowed "only if needed"**: use them for **keywords** (*"climate → water projects → UCY course"*), not full sentences.

> [!tip] How to avoid memorising a script
> Rehearse from **keywords**, not from a written text. Each time you rehearse, the sentences come out slightly differently. That's good: it means you're *explaining*, not *reciting*.

## 10. Delivery

*"Look animated. Be expressive. Speak to the audience."*

| Area | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | Speak **loudly and clearly**. Slow down; pause between goals. |
| **Eye contact** | Look at the **audience**, not the screen or the floor. Move your eyes around the whole room. |
| **Stage presence** | **Stand confidently** and use the space. Don't hide behind the laptop. |
| **Body language** | **Minimise nervous gestures** (playing with a pen, hair or pockets; swaying). Use your hands to support what you say. |
| **English only** | Keep the whole presentation in English, including asides like "sorry, one second". |

> [!tip] Handling nerves and mistakes
> - **Forgot what to say?** Look at the slide's keywords. That's exactly what they're for. Pause, breathe, continue.
> - **Made a grammar mistake?** Correct it briefly (*"…I mean, I **have** been interested…"*) or just continue. Don't apologise repeatedly.
> - **Laptop/clicker problem?** Keep talking about the goal while it's fixed. Your talk doesn't depend on the slides.
> - Have a **backup**: the file on a USB stick **and** in your email/cloud, saved as `.pptx` (and maybe also as PDF).

## 11. Using AI

AI **may help with preparation**, but it **cannot replace your understanding**.

| ✅ Appropriate support | ❌ Not acceptable |
|---|---|
| Brainstorming ideas for goals | Using AI as the **factual source** |
| Checking vocabulary or grammar | Reading an **AI-written script** |
| Organising slide order | Including information **you can't explain** |
| Improving visual clarity | **Copying without checking** |

> [!important] What's graded
> *"The grade comes from what the student can explain live in class."* If the professor asks you about something on your slide and you can't explain it, it hurts both your *Research & Explanation* and *Content* marks.

> [!warning] Trick case: AI-polished vocabulary
> If AI rewrites your text with words you don't normally use (*"I aspire to leverage my multifaceted skill set…"*), it sounds unnatural when spoken, and you may mispronounce or misuse the words. Use vocabulary you **can say comfortably and explain**.

## 12. Grading Rubric (10 marks)

Five criteria, **2 marks each**:

| # | Criterion | What's assessed | How to get 2/2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Content & Development** | four clear goals + meaningful explanation | All **4** required categories, each with a clear ambition **and** a real *why/how*, not just "I like it" |
| 2 | **Organisation & PowerPoint** | logical structure + clear supporting slides | Intro → 4 goals → conclusion → references; consistent, simple, visual slides; no spelling mistakes |
| 3 | **Spoken Language** | B2 vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation | Varied future forms and linking words (see Language Bank); accurate grammar; clear pronunciation |
| 4 | **Delivery & Communication** | clear speech, audience contact, limited reading | Loud, clear, eye contact, confident posture, minimal reading |
| 5 | **Research, Explanation & APA** | relevant facts, source citation, own words | One relevant fact per goal, paraphrased, cited (Author, Year), References slide in APA 7 |

> [!warning] Easy ways to lose marks
> - **Only 3 goals**, or two goals from the same category → Content.
> - A goal **with no researched fact** → Research.
> - **No References slide**, or citations that don't match it → Research/APA and Organisation.
> - **Reading** from slides or a script → Delivery **and** Spoken Language (reading hides your real speaking level).
> - **Spelling mistakes** on slides → Organisation & PowerPoint.
> - **Going far over or under** 4–5 minutes. Not a criterion by itself, but it usually signals weak development (too short) or reading too much (too long).

## 13. Language Bank (B2)

Using a **variety** of structures helps your *Spoken Language* mark.

### Opening
- *"Good morning, everyone. My name is … and today I'm going to talk about my future goals."*
- *"I'll present four goals: an academic goal, a career goal, a hobby, and one personal goal."*

### Talking about the future (vary these)
| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| would like to / I'd love to | *I'd like to study abroad.* |
| be planning to / be hoping to | *I'm planning to apply for an internship next summer.* |
| intend to / aim to | *I aim to finish my degree in four years.* |
| be going to (decided plan) | *I'm going to take an IELTS course.* |
| My dream / goal / ambition is to + verb | *My ambition is to work for a tech company.* |
| **Future perfect** (B2): by + time | *By 2030, I **will have finished** my master's.* |
| **Future continuous** (B2) | *In five years, I **will be working** as an engineer.* |
| **First conditional** | *If I get good grades, I will apply for a scholarship.* |
| **Second conditional** (less likely goals) | *If I had more free time, I would learn the piano.* |

### Giving reasons and explaining how
- *The main reason is that… / This matters to me because… / What motivates me is…*
- *In order to achieve this, I will need to… / The first step is… / After that,…*
- *I've always been interested in… / Since I was a child,…*

### Moving between goals
- *Moving on to my career goal,… / My second goal is related to… / Now, let me talk about…*
- *On a more personal note,…* (for the Free Choice goal)

### Concluding
- *To sum up, my four goals are… / All of these goals are connected because…*
- *Thank you for listening. Are there any questions?*

> [!warning] Common language mistakes
> | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
> |---|---|
> | I want to become **engineer**. | I want to become **an** engineer. |
> | I'm interested **for / to** programming. | I'm interested **in** programming. |
> | I look forward to **work** abroad. | I look forward to **working** abroad. *(to + -ing)* |
> | **In the future I will to** study. | In the future, I **will study** / I **want to study**. |
> | I will make a master. | I will **do / complete** a master's (degree). |
> | **According to me**,… | **In my opinion**,… *("according to" is for sources)* |
> | The programme **learns** students… | The programme **teaches** students… |
> | I have **interest** about travelling. | I'm **interested in** travelling / I **have an interest in** travelling. |
> | **Since 5 years** I play guitar. | I **have been playing** guitar **for** 5 years. |
> | **Informations / researches** | **information / research** *(uncountable)* |

## 14. Common Mistakes

> [!warning] Summary of the most common mistakes
> 1. **Text-heavy slides** that you then read. Use keywords only.
> 2. **Missing the research** on one or more goals. Every goal needs one documented fact.
> 3. **Citing ChatGPT** or Google, or giving a bare URL. Cite the real page in APA format.
> 4. **No References slide**, or in-text citations that don't match it.
> 5. **Vague goals** ("be happy", "travel"). Make them specific.
> 6. **All "why", no "how"**: explain *how* you plan to achieve each goal too.
> 7. **Copying the source's sentence** instead of paraphrasing.
> 8. **Not timing yourself out loud**, so you end up under 4 or over 5 minutes.
> 9. **Spelling mistakes** on slides.
> 10. Including information (often from AI) that you **can't explain** if asked.

## 15. Preparation Timeline

| When | What |
|---|---|
| **Step 1** | Choose your 4 goals (one per category). Write one sentence for each. |
| **Step 2** | For each goal, write 2–3 reasons (*why*) and 1–2 steps (*how*). |
| **Step 3** | Research: find **one** reliable source per goal. Save the URL, author, date and page title. |
| **Step 4** | Write the APA references and in-text citations. |
| **Step 5** | Build the slides: title, intro, 4 goals, conclusion, references. Add visuals. Spell-check. |
| **Step 6** | Make keyword prompt cards (optional). |
| **Step 7** | Rehearse **out loud, standing, timed**, at least 3–4 times, ideally once in front of someone. |
| **Step 8** | Final check (below) and back up the file. |

## 16. Final Checklist

From the professor's "Before presentation day" slide, plus the practical extras:

- [ ] I have **four required goals** (Academic, Career, Hobby/Interest, Free Choice).
- [ ] Each goal has a clear **ambition**, an **explanation** (why + how), and **one researched fact**.
- [ ] Each source is **reliable** (not AI, not a blog) and **cited in APA style** on the slide.
- [ ] I have a final **References** slide: alphabetical, hanging indent, matches the in-text citations.
- [ ] My slides are **simple and visual**: keywords only, relevant images, no spelling mistakes.
- [ ] I can **explain everything without reading**, including every fact and source.
- [ ] My presentation lasts **4–5 minutes** (timed out loud).
- [ ] Everything is in **English**.
- [ ] I have a **backup** of the file (USB + email/cloud).

> [!quote] Final message from the professor
> *Your slides show the key information. You explain the story behind them.*
